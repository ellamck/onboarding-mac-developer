# Data Privacy and Confidentiality

## Research & Learn

**Review Focus Bear’s privacy policy and note key takeaways. (Hint: The privacy policy is available here.)**

- Focus Bear follows the EU's GDPR, and is responsible for how user's personal data is collected and used
- Details are anonymised for user protections, and data is only collected when absolutely needed
- Focus Bear uses third-party providers, and the five services will also collect, use and store Focus Bear user's personal data along with the processors
- Users can email privacy@focusbear.io to exercise their rights from the GDPR
- Focus Bear will only process data for its explicit, intended purpose

**What types of data are considered confidential at Focus Bear?**

- Identification data (email addresses, phone numbers for example)
- Social data (habit data for example)
- Academic and professional data (occupation for example)
- Commercial and marketing data (Whatsapp number, email, for example)
- Personal device details (technical data)
- Economic, financial and insurance data (payment details, preferences, etc.)
- Profile data (preferences, opt-ins, etc.)
- Agreggate data (agreggated data for statistical information, e.g. country based on IP address)
- Religious beliefs and health related data

**What are best practices for handling confidential data?**
Encrypting and anonymising the data, and limiting the privilege to the data (only trusted third party or processor can view payment details, only team members who need to communicate will be able to view a user's email, etc.). Understanding what data may be confidential is also important, and being transparent with the individual(s) so they can fully understand what data is being collected (“Principles for Managing Confidentiality | Department of Finance,” 2025).

Like many of the guidelines in GDPR, data collection and processing should be limited to the strict purposes (University of Delaware, 2018). Data should be stored securely and disposed of securely.

**How should you respond to a suspected data breach or accidental disclosure of confidential information?**
The outlined steps from Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC, 2025):

1. Contain the breach to make sure it does not spread.
2. Evaluate the risks that have occurred, what harms have affected individuals, and make sure no further harm occurs to the best of your company's ability.
3. Notify the affected individuals and the relevant reporting parties, different regulations are in effect if it qualifies as an 'eligible data breach' (OAIC, 2025). The GDPR indicates there should be no undue delay in this notification, and that the communication should be in lay language so it is easy to understand, much like Focus Bear's privacy policy (Intersoft Consulting, 2013).
4. Review what happened, and understand what can be implemented to prevent it from happening again. Due diligence to prevent and mediate the breach must be put in place.

**How should you store, share, and dispose of sensitive information safely?**

- Data should be classified and labeled for a specific use (“Storing, Using, Sharing Data | Digital.NSW,” n.d.)
- Before sharing data, it should be anonymised and encrypted (Blias, 2026)
- Data should be stored securely (“Storing, Using, Sharing Data | Digital.NSW,” n.d.) and backed up regularly (Blias, 2026)
- When sharing data, users should understand the risks involved (Guidelines for Data Transfers | Cyber.Gov.Au, 2026)
- When importing data, the data should be first scanned for malicious content, and when exporting data, the export should have to be authorised prior to the export (Guidelines for Data Transfers | Cyber.Gov.Au, 2026)
- All data transfers should also be logged for traceability (Guidelines for Data Transfers | Cyber.Gov.Au, 2026)
- For secure deletions, I found the following from the University of Edinburgh (“Secure Deletion,” 2024):
  - For physical storage devices that use magnets, use a secure deletion tool to overwrite all the data several times
  - For flash drives and SSDs, use the manufacturer's secure deletion method, and delete decryption keys
  - For physical data, dispose of paper documents with a cross-cut shredder

**What are some common mistakes that lead to data privacy issues, and how can they be avoided?**
This section is derived from OAIC's tips for good privacy practices (OAIC, 2023):

1. Weak Access Controls
   Workplace policies should implement strong access controls, ensuring that only just enough access is provided.

2. Unknown Third-Parties and Other Vendors
   Know the privacy practices of the Processors and Third-Parties, and ensure that the data shared will be handled appropriately, meeting guidelines and secure practices.

3. Phishing, Human Error
   Strong password management, multifactor authentication and strong network security can mitigate human error.

4. No Encryption or Anonymisation
   De-identify as much data as possible, and dispose of data securely as soon as possible.

5. Non-Purposeful Storage of Data
   Only store data that has an intended, justifiable purpose for the timeframe. Dispose of and avoid collecting unnecessary data.

6. Indirect Collection of Personal Data
   Personal data should always be collected directly so the individual is informed of what they are sharing and that they do not have to share the data (they can consent).
